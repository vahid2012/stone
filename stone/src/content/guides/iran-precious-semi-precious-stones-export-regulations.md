# Iran Precious and Semi-Precious Stones Export Regulations: Turquoise and Agate

Iran is home to some of the world's most famous precious and semi-precious stones. Neyshabur turquoise, prized for its distinctive blue color, has been mined in northeastern Iran for over 2,000 years. Iranian agate from Kerman and other regions is also highly valued in international gem and mineral markets. However, exporting these stones involves a different regulatory framework than dimension stone. This guide explains the classification, permit requirements, documentation, and market access considerations for exporting Iranian turquoise and agate to international buyers.

## Stone Classification in Iran's Regulatory Framework

Iran's mining and mineral regulations distinguish between several categories of stone, each governed by different rules. Understanding where your product falls is the first step in determining the applicable export regulations.

**Dimension stone** includes marble, granite, travertine, limestone, and onyx — stones quarried in large blocks for construction and architectural use. These are the most commonly exported Iranian stones and fall under HS chapter 25. Dimension stone exports are generally straightforward, with most types exempt from export duties.

**Decorative stone** includes travertine and marble in smaller, ornamental formats, as well as some onyx varieties used for decorative objects and small architectural elements. These may overlap with dimension stone in classification but can have different permit requirements depending on format and value.

**Precious and semi-precious stones** include turquoise, agate, jasper, and various other gem and ornamental minerals. These fall under a separate regulatory regime, typically classified under HS chapter 71 (natural or cultured pearls, precious or semi-precious stones, precious metals). The export of these stones is subject to stricter oversight because of their higher unit value, potential use in jewelry, and the involvement of Iran's gem and mineral heritage.

Turquoise from Neyshabur and agate from Kerman are the two most internationally recognized Iranian semi-precious stones. Their export involves considerations that do not apply to dimension stone — including special permits, valuation scrutiny, and gemological certification.

## Neyshabur Turquoise: Source and Characteristics

The Neyshabur turquoise mines, located in Razavi Khorasan province in northeastern Iran, are among the oldest and most famous turquoise sources in the world. The mines have been worked for over two millennia, and Neyshabur turquoise is renowned for its deep blue color, often described as "robin's egg blue" or "Persian blue."

Neyshabur turquoise is classified into several grades:

- **Angleshah**: The highest grade, with a pure, intense blue color and no matrix. This is the most valuable variety and is prized by jewelry makers and collectors worldwide.
- **Baqqi**: A medium-grade turquoise with a lighter blue color and some matrix veining. Suitable for jewelry and decorative use.
- **Jahrdari**: Lower-grade turquoise with significant matrix and more greenish tones. Used for beads, inlay work, and lower-end decorative applications.

The Neyshabur turquoise deposit is managed by IMIDRO (Iranian Mines and Mining Industries Development and Renovation Organization), which oversees mining operations and regulates production. Because turquoise is a high-value gemstone, its extraction, processing, and export are more closely monitored than dimension stone.

## Kerman Agate: Source and Characteristics

Kerman province in southeastern Iran is a major source of agate — a form of chalcedony that occurs in a range of colors and patterns. Kerman agate is known for its banding, translucency, and variety of colors, including red, brown, white, and multi-colored specimens.

Kerman agate is used in:

- **Jewelry**: Cabochons, beads, and carved pieces for rings, pendants, and bracelets.
- **Decorative objects**: Carved bowls, spheres, and ornamental pieces.
- **Mineral collections**: Specimen-grade agate geodes and nodules sought by collectors.

The agate deposits around Kerman are worked by both formal mining operations and artisanal miners. This mixed production base can complicate export compliance, as artisanal material may lack formal documentation. Buyers should ensure that their supplier can provide proper origin documentation and that the material is legally sourced.

## Export Permit Requirements

Precious and semi-precious stone exports from Iran may require special permits that go beyond the standard export documentation. The key regulatory bodies involved are:

**IMIDRO** (Iranian Mines and Mining Industries Development and Renovation Organization): IMIDRO oversees Iran's mining sector and may require export approval for certain minerals, particularly high-value gemstones. For turquoise, which is extracted from IMIDRO-managed mines, export authorization is typically required.

**Ministry of Industry, Mine and Trade (MIMT)**: The ministry sets broader export policy, including any quantity restrictions, valuation thresholds, or prohibitions that may apply to specific mineral categories. Policy changes can affect which stones require permits and under what conditions.

The permit process varies by stone type and value. For lower-value agate in commercial quantities, the requirements may be minimal. For high-value turquoise — particularly Angleshah grade — the process is more rigorous and may involve valuation review to ensure the declared value reflects the stone's true market worth.

Your supplier's customs broker is the best source of current permit requirements. Regulations can change, and the specific requirements depend on the stone type, grade, quantity, and destination. Before committing to a purchase, confirm with the supplier that they can obtain all necessary permits for your shipment.

## HS Codes and Customs Classification

Correct HS code classification is critical for both export clearance and import clearance at the destination. Precious and semi-precious stones fall under HS chapter 71, but the specific sub-codes depend on the stone type and form.

Key classifications include:

- **HS 7103.10**: Precious stones (other than diamonds), unworked or simply sawn.
- **HS 7103.91**: Worked precious stones (other than diamonds).
- **HS 7106.10**: Precious and semi-precious stones (unworked).
- **HS 7106.92**: Worked precious and semi-precious stones.

Turquoise and agate may be classified under HS 7103 or HS 7106 depending on whether they are considered "precious" or "semi-precious" and whether they are worked or unworked. The distinction matters because different codes may have different duty rates and permit requirements in both Iran and the destination country.

Incorrect classification can lead to customs delays, duty reassessments, or even seizure of goods. The customs broker determines the correct code based on the specific stone type, its form (rough, worked, or finished), and current Iranian customs tariff schedules.

## Gemological Certification

While not always legally required, gemological certification is strongly recommended for higher-value turquoise and agate exports. A certificate from an accredited gemological laboratory provides:

- **Authenticity verification**: Confirms that the stone is natural turquoise or agate (not synthetic or treated).
- **Quality grading**: Documents the stone's color, clarity, and any treatments.
- **Valuation support**: Provides an independent assessment that supports the declared value for customs purposes.
- **Buyer confidence**: Enhances marketability, particularly for jewelry-grade material sold to discerning buyers.

Accredited gemological laboratories that issue certificates for Iranian stones include the **GIA** (Gemological Institute of America), **Gübelin Gem Lab** (Switzerland), and various national gemological institutes. For stones processed in Iran, the **Iran Gemological Institute** can provide preliminary certification, though international buyers often prefer certification from a globally recognized laboratory.

For high-value shipments (particularly Angleshah-grade turquoise), certification should be arranged before export. The certificate accompanies the shipment and is presented to destination customs if requested.

## Destination Import Regulations

Importing precious and semi-precious stones into your destination country may involve requirements beyond standard customs procedures. Common requirements include:

- **Gemological certificates**: Some countries require certification for imports above a certain value threshold.
- **Valuation reports**: Customs authorities may require an independent valuation to assess import duties, particularly for high-value turquoise.
- **Import licenses**: A few countries require specific licenses for importing gemstones, especially for commercial quantities.
- **Kimberley Process considerations**: While the Kimberley Process applies to diamonds, some customs authorities apply enhanced scrutiny to all gemstone imports from certain regions.

Before shipping, research your destination country's import requirements for turquoise and agate. Your customs broker at the destination can confirm what is needed. For EU destinations, CE marking does not apply to gemstones, but product safety regulations for jewelry may apply if the stones are sold as finished jewelry components.

## How to Navigate Iran's Precious and Semi-Precious Stone Export Regulations

### 1. Identify the stone classification

Iran classifies stones as dimension stone, decorative stone, or precious/semi-precious. Turquoise (Neyshabur) and agate (Kerman) fall under a different regulatory regime than travertine or marble. Confirm the classification with your supplier, as this determines which permits, HS codes, and procedures apply to your shipment.

### 2. Check export permit requirements

Precious and semi-precious stone exports may require special permits from the Iranian Mines and Mining Industries Development and Renovation Organization (IMIDRO) or the Ministry of Industry, Mine and Trade. Your supplier's customs broker confirms which permits apply and begins the application process. Allow extra time for permit processing, as it can take longer than standard export documentation.

### 3. Verify quantity and value restrictions

Some semi-precious stones have export quantity limits or minimum value thresholds. Confirm current regulations with your supplier's customs broker before planning a shipment. For high-value turquoise, customs may conduct a valuation review to ensure the declared value is accurate and not understated.

### 4. Obtain gemological certificates

For higher-value turquoise and agate, gemological certification from an accredited laboratory enhances marketability and may be required by buyers or destination customs. Arrange certification before export for high-value shipments. Keep the original certificate with the shipment documents.

### 5. Prepare standard export documents

In addition to any special permits, you need a commercial invoice, packing list, certificate of origin, and export declaration. Ensure the HS code for precious/semi-precious stones is correctly declared. All document values must be consistent and reflect the true market value of the stones.

### 6. Confirm destination import regulations

Some countries have specific import requirements for precious and semi-precious stones, including gemological certificates, valuation reports, or import licenses. Check before shipping. Your destination customs broker can confirm requirements and help you prepare the necessary import documentation.
