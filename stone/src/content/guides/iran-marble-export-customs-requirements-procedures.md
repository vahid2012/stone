# Iran Marble Export Customs Clearance Requirements and Procedures

Customs clearance is a critical step in exporting marble from Iran. If documents are incomplete or incorrect, your shipment can be delayed at Bandar Abbas — adding costs and disrupting your project timeline. A well-organized supplier with an experienced customs broker can navigate the process efficiently, but understanding the requirements yourself helps you anticipate issues and verify that everything is on track. This guide walks through every document, procedure, and checkpoint involved in clearing Iranian marble through customs for export.

## Understanding Iran's Customs Framework for Stone Exports

Iran's customs authority, operating under the Islamic Republic of Iran Customs Administration (IRICA), regulates all exports leaving the country. Marble and other dimension stones fall under HS code 2515 (marble, travertine, and alabaster) and HS code 251512 (marble blocks). The regulatory framework requires exporters to file a formal export declaration, present supporting documents, and obtain a clearance permit before any container can enter the port and be loaded onto a vessel.

The primary export hub for Iranian stone is Bandar Abbas, located on the Persian Gulf in Hormozgan province. Most marble quarried in Iran — whether from Dehbid in Fars province, Harsin in Kermanshah, or Markazi province — is trucked to Bandar Abbas for containerized export. The port handles thousands of stone containers monthly, and its customs office is well-versed in stone export procedures. However, the volume of traffic means that even minor document discrepancies can cause significant delays.

## Required Documents for Marble Export Clearance

The documentation package for marble export from Iran is well-defined. Every shipment requires the following:

- **Commercial Invoice**: Must include the stone type, HS code, unit price, total value, buyer and seller details, and Incoterms. The currency is typically US dollars or euros.
- **Packing List**: Lists block or slab dimensions, quantities, weights (gross and net), and container number. For block shipments, individual block dimensions and weights are essential.
- **Certificate of Origin**: Issued by the Iran Chamber of Commerce, Cooperatives, and Guilds. This confirms the marble was quarried in Iran and is critical for destination customs, especially when preferential tariffs apply.
- **Export Declaration (SAD Form)**: The Single Administrative Document filed with IRICA through the EPL (Electronic Processing of Declarations) system. The customs broker submits this electronically.
- **Quality/Test Certificates**: Depending on the destination, buyers may request additional certificates — such as radioactivity test reports for EU-bound stone or petrographic analysis certificates.

All documents must be consistent. The commercial invoice value, packing list weights, and export declaration figures must match exactly. Even a small discrepancy — such as a weight difference of a few kilograms between the packing list and the export declaration — can trigger a customs query and delay the shipment.

## The Bandar Abbas Clearance Process

The clearance process at Bandar Abbas follows a structured sequence. First, the supplier or their customs broker files the export declaration electronically through the EPL system. IRICA reviews the declaration and cross-references it against the supporting documents. If everything is in order, the system issues a provisional acceptance.

Next, the container is brought to the port's customs inspection area. Physical inspections are not always required — IRICA uses a risk-based system that selects a percentage of containers for random or targeted inspection. If your container is selected, customs officers may open it to verify that the contents match the declared goods. For marble shipments, this typically involves confirming block count, dimensions, and visual confirmation of stone type.

Once the declaration is approved (with or without inspection), the customs broker pays any applicable duties and fees. A clearance permit is then issued, allowing the container to proceed to the terminal for vessel loading. The shipping line issues the bill of lading after loading, typically within 24–48 hours of the vessel's departure.

## Common Causes of Customs Delays

Despite a well-established process, delays do occur. Understanding the most common causes helps you and your supplier prevent them:

**Document discrepancies** are the leading cause of delays. A mismatch between the commercial invoice value and the export declaration, a misspelled buyer name, or an incorrect HS code can all trigger a customs hold. The customs broker must then file a correction, which adds 1–3 business days.

**Incomplete certificates** are another frequent issue. If the certificate of origin lacks a required stamp or authentication, or if a quality certificate is missing for a destination that requires it, customs will not release the container.

**Random inspections** are unavoidable and can add 1–2 days. While you cannot prevent being selected, having documents perfectly in order ensures the inspection goes smoothly.

**Port congestion** at Bandar Abbas, particularly during peak shipping seasons (March–May and September–November), can extend the overall timeline. Booking vessel space in advance and coordinating container delivery to the port early helps mitigate this.

## Working with a Customs Broker

A competent customs broker is your most valuable asset in the clearance process. Iranian customs brokers are licensed by IRICA and specialize in specific product categories and ports. A broker experienced with stone exports understands the specific HS codes, common document issues, and how to navigate the EPL system efficiently.

Your supplier typically has an established relationship with a customs broker who handles their regular shipments. However, as a buyer, you should confirm that the broker has stone export experience and ask about their typical clearance timeline. A good broker can often complete clearance in 2–3 business days for straightforward shipments.

If you are working under EXW terms — meaning you, the buyer, are responsible for export clearance — you will need to engage your own customs broker in Iran. This is less common for international buyers, and most prefer FOB or CIF terms where the supplier handles export clearance.

## Incoterms and Who Handles Clearance

The Incoterms under which you purchase determine who is responsible for export customs clearance. This is a critical consideration:

- **FOB Bandar Abbas**: The supplier handles all export procedures, customs clearance, and loading. The buyer takes responsibility once the goods are on board the vessel. This is the most common term for Iranian stone exports.
- **CIF (Cost, Insurance, Freight)**: The supplier handles export clearance and ocean freight to the destination port. The buyer handles import clearance at the destination.
- **EXW (Ex Works)**: The buyer is responsible for everything from the quarry to the final destination, including Iranian export customs clearance. This requires the buyer to have a representative or customs broker in Iran.

Most international buyers prefer FOB or CIF terms, as managing export clearance from abroad can be complex. For more on this topic, see our guide on [Incoterms for stone export from Iran: FOB, CIF, and EXW](/guides/incoterms-stone-export-iran-fob-cif-exw/).

## Duty and Tax Considerations

One of the advantages of sourcing marble from Iran is that most dimension stone exports are exempt from export duties. The Iranian government actively encourages non-oil exports, and stone is a significant category. However, regulations can change, and certain processed or semi-processed stone products may have applicable fees.

The customs broker confirms current duty rates at the time of filing. If duties apply, they are calculated based on the declared value of the shipment. It is important that the commercial invoice reflects a realistic market value — customs authorities compare declared values against known market prices for similar stone types.

In addition to duties, there are standard customs processing fees and port handling charges. These are relatively modest — typically a few hundred dollars per container — and are usually included in the FOB price quoted by the supplier.

## How to Complete Iran Marble Export Customs Clearance

### 1. Prepare the commercial invoice and packing list

Your supplier prepares a commercial invoice with stone type, HS code, value, and buyer details, plus a packing list with block dimensions and weights. These must be accurate and consistent. Any discrepancy between these two documents — even a minor weight difference — can trigger a customs hold. Double-check that the buyer's company name, address, and contact details match exactly across all documents.

### 2. Obtain the certificate of origin from the Chamber of Commerce

The Iran Chamber of Commerce issues the certificate of origin, confirming the marble was quarried in Iran. This typically takes 2–3 business days. The supplier submits the commercial invoice and a declaration of origin to the Chamber, which issues the certificate with an official stamp. For certain destinations, additional legalization by the destination country's embassy in Iran may be required.

### 3. Submit the export declaration to Iran Customs

The supplier or their customs broker files the export declaration (SAD form) with Iran Customs, including all shipment details and supporting documents. This is done electronically through the EPL system. The declaration includes the HS code, shipment value, weight, buyer information, and vessel details. The customs broker ensures all fields are correctly completed before submission.

### 4. Pay export duties if applicable

Most stone exports from Iran are exempt from export duties, but some stone types may have applicable fees. Your supplier's customs broker handles this. If duties or fees apply, they are paid at the time of declaration approval. The broker provides a receipt of payment, which becomes part of the clearance documentation.

### 5. Obtain customs clearance for loading

Once customs approves the declaration, a clearance permit is issued allowing the container to enter the port and be loaded onto the vessel. The trucking company transporting the container from the quarry or processing facility to Bandar Abbas presents this permit at the port gate. Without it, the container cannot enter the terminal.

### 6. Confirm loading and receive the bill of lading

After the container is loaded at Bandar Abbas, the shipping line issues the bill of lading. Your supplier sends all original documents to you for destination customs clearance. The bill of lading is the key document for claiming your goods at the destination port and should be checked carefully for accuracy — vessel name, container number, seal number, and consignee details.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What documents are needed for Iran marble export customs clearance?

You need a commercial invoice, packing list, certificate of origin from the Iran Chamber of Commerce, export declaration (SAD form), and any required quality or compliance certificates. For EU destinations, a radioactivity test report may also be required.

### How long does customs clearance take for marble exports from Iran?

Customs clearance at Bandar Abbas typically takes 2–5 business days if all documents are correct. Delays occur when documents have discrepancies or when additional inspections are required. Working with an experienced customs broker can often reduce clearance time to 2–3 days.

### Are there export duties on marble from Iran?

Most dimension stone exports from Iran are exempt from export duties, but regulations can change and some stone types may have applicable fees. Your supplier's customs broker confirms current rates at the time of filing. Standard customs processing and port fees still apply.

### Who handles customs clearance for marble exports — the buyer or the supplier?

Under FOB Bandar Abbas, the supplier handles export customs clearance. Under EXW, the buyer is responsible. Under CIF, the supplier handles export clearance and the buyer handles import clearance at destination. Most international buyers prefer FOB or CIF terms.

### What causes delays in Iran marble export customs clearance?

Common causes include document discrepancies (mismatched values, names, or HS codes), incomplete certificates, or random customs inspections. A well-organized supplier with an experienced customs broker minimizes these risks. Port congestion during peak seasons can also extend timelines.

### Can I track my container after it clears Iran customs?

Yes. Once the container clears customs and is loaded at Bandar Abbas, you can track it using the bill of lading number or container number on the shipping line's website or a container tracking platform such as MSC, Hapag-Lloyd, or Maersk's tracking portals.
